1. Laboratory Safety & Tools

Safety rules

  • General: read instructions first; no horseplay; never work unsupervised or do unauthorized experiments; no eating/drinking/gum; keep aisles clear; don't sit on lab tables.
  • Personal: approved eye protection, lab gown, gloves, closed-toe shoes, tied-back hair, no jewelry or long nails; wash hands before and after; report all accidents, spills and breakages immediately.
  • Chemicals: treat all as hazardous; read labels; label every container; never pour chemicals back into the original container; don't touch broken glass (use tools); don't put hot glassware directly on surfaces; turn off heat sources.

MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheet)

Document from the manufacturer/supplier describing a chemical and how to handle it safely. Sections: chemical identification; hazard information (signal words Danger/Warning); composition; first aid; firefighting; accidental release; handling & storage; exposure controls/PPE; stability & reactivity; toxicology. Purpose: protects users and guides emergency response.

Course lab routines

  • Wear lab gown at all times and when borrowing tools; use the white and pink forms to borrow tools and request reagents; keep a lab notebook (checked at semester end).
  • Each group keeps one green clear book (group name, year, block, members) for lab reports; submit reports on time.
  • Wash hands before and after; wear mask when needed; minimize moving between tables; tell the professor if unwell; tie hair, no long nails or jewelry, closed shoes.
  • Group lab kit: tissue, cloth/towel, rag, sponge, dishwashing liquid, tape, scissors, marker, 6-L plastic container. Personal kit: hand towel, extra shirt, alcohol, soap, gloves, lab gown, hairnet.

Care of tools: keep clean, dry, organized and labeled; calibrate regularly; do preventive maintenance; repair faulty equipment promptly.

2. Biochemistry: Nature, Scope & Elements of Life

  • Term "biochemistry" coined by Neuberg (1903) from Greek bios (life) + chymos (juice).
  • Study of chemical processes in living things at the molecular level; links biology and chemistry (structure, mechanisms, function of biomolecules).
  • Four biomolecules: proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleic acids.
  • Relevance to nursing: metabolism, enzymes, DNA/RNA, cell membranes; disease mechanisms (diabetes, cancer, cardiovascular), genetic disorders, pathophysiology; pharmacology (drug mechanisms, metabolism, therapeutic drug monitoring, correct dosage); nutrition and malnutrition markers; diagnostic tests, sample handling, patient monitoring.
  • Florence Nightingale ("Lady with the Lamp", b. 12 May 1820, d. 13 Aug 1910): pioneer of modern nursing, hospital hygiene and statistics (polar area diagram).

  • Cell wall: outside the membrane, mainly cellulose; supports and protects, gives box-like shape.

Prokaryotes: no nucleus (e.g., bacteria, ~10× smaller than a human skin cell). Eukaryotes: nucleus + organelles.

4. Water

Functions in the body

Building material of every cell; solvent and transport (nutrients, oxygen, via blood); regulates temperature (sweating, respiration); flushes waste (urine); shock absorber for brain, spinal cord, fetus; forms saliva; lubricates joints; keeps mucosa moist; needed for digestion.

Distribution

  • ~75% of Earth's surface; ~97% of water is in oceans; ~3% is fresh, two-thirds of which is locked in ice caps/glaciers.
  • Water is ~70% of human body mass: blood plasma 92%, muscle 80%, red blood cell 60%. Babies/kids have a higher percentage than adults; women lower than men; more fat tissue = lower percentage.

Structure

  • O–H bond length 0.096 nm; H–O–H angle ~105° (104.5°); bent/nonlinear.
  • Electronegativity: O = 3.5, H = 2.1 → polar O–H bonds (δ− on O, δ+ on H). The bent shape makes the whole molecule polar (dipole moment 1.855 D).
  • Liquid/solid water molecules are linked by hydrogen bonds.

Why ice floats: cooling to 4 °C increases density; from 4 → 0 °C water expands into an open, structured arrangement; freezing expands volume ~9%, so ice is less dense than liquid water.

Meniscus: curve of liquid in a glass cylinder. Water's is concave because adhesion (water–glass) > cohesion (water–water).

Electrolytes

Minerals that carry an electric charge (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) in blood, urine and sweat. Sweat always contains Na and Cl; it is saltier at higher sweat rates. Sweat glands reabsorb some salt.

Hydrotherapy: use of water for healing (warm baths, cold immersion, aquatic exercise, steam/sauna).

5. Acids, Bases, pH & Buffers

  • Water is the universal solvent: dissolves more substances than any other liquid.
  • Acid raises H⁺ (sour, turns blue litmus red, reacts with metals to release H₂, reacts with bases to form salts). Base raises OH⁻ (bitter, slippery, turns red litmus blue).
TheoryAcidBase
Arrhenius (1884)Excess H⁺ in waterExcess OH⁻ in water
Brønsted–Lowry (1923)Proton (H⁺) donorProton acceptor
Lewis (1923)Electron-pair acceptorElectron-pair donor
  • pH scale 0–14: 7 neutral, <7 acidic, >7 basic; logarithmic, each step = tenfold change (pH 4 is 10× more acidic than pH 5).
  • Examples: vinegar 2–3; Coke 2.6–2.7 (phosphoric acid acidulant); coffee ≈5; milk ≈6; natural soaps 8–10; shampoo 3.5–9; toothpaste 7–10. Normal vaginal pH 3.8–5. Soda acids: carbonic, phosphoric, citric.
  • Buffer = resists pH change; usually a weak acid + its conjugate base. Blood buffer: carbonic acid–bicarbonate.
  • Blood pH 7.35–7.45, kept by three systems: blood buffers, lungs, kidneys.
  • Acidosis (too much acid): mild = confusion/disorientation; extreme = shock, coma, death. Alkalosis (too much base): mild = irregular breathing, muscle contraction; extreme = convulsions, death.
  • Safe drinking water pH 6.5–8.5. Too acidic corrodes pipes/leaches lead; too basic tastes bitter. Carbonate ions buffer natural water.

6. Diffusion & Osmosis

  • Diffusion: movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration.
  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from higher water concentration to lower water concentration (toward higher solute).
  • Importance: water homeostasis, nutrient entry, waste removal, pressure balance inside/outside cells. Plants absorb water via roots; animal cells distribute nutrients and remove wastes.
  • Examples: thirst after salty food, kidney dialysis, raisins swelling in water, wrinkled skin after long soaking.
  • Hyponatremia: low blood sodium (cells take in too much water); causes nausea, vomiting, fatigue, headache, confusion.
  • Dialysis: blood passes through semipermeable tubes surrounded by dialysis fluid flowing in the opposite direction; removes waste and excess water; 4–5 hours per session.

7. Molecular Structure & Polarity

  • Molecular structure = placement of atoms only (lone pairs not counted); electron-pair geometry counts bonds and lone pairs (VSEPR).
  • Central atom: lowest subscript / forms most bonds; if tied, least electronegative.

Bond typeEN differenceDescription
Nonpolar covalent0 to 0.4Equal sharing: H₂, O₂, N₂; C–H and C–C in alkanes, alkenes, alkynes (C 2.55, H 2.20) → hydrocarbons nonpolar
Polar covalent0.5 to 1.9Unequal sharing; δ+ on lower EN atom, δ− on higher EN atom; e.g., H₂O
Ionic2.0 and aboveTransfer of electrons
  • SO₂ is polar: polar S=O bonds and a bent shape (unlike linear CO₂). Dipole moment measures molecular polarity. Hydrogen bonding occurs when H interacts with highly electronegative F, O or N.
  • Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) and superheavy synthetic elements lack Pauling EN values (Kr 3.00 and Xe 2.60 do have values).
  • Bonding: sharing e⁻ = covalent; donating = ionic; delocalizing = metallic. Atoms bond to reach stability (octet rule).
  • Electronegativity = pull on shared electrons. Polarity = charge separation from EN difference; gives a dipole moment; affects boiling/melting point and solubility.
  • Polar molecule test: (1) Lewis structure → (2) electron geometry → (3) molecular geometry → (4) find polar bonds → (5) check whether dipoles cancel. Symmetric shapes with identical terminal atoms (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral) cancel = nonpolar; asymmetric (bent, pyramidal, T-shaped) or lone pairs on the central atom = polar.
  • H₂O polar (bent); CO₂ nonpolar despite polar C=O bonds; XeF₂ nonpolar; CCl₄ nonpolar despite polar C–Cl bonds.

8. Organic Chemistry

Fundamentals

  • Study of carbon compounds. Carbon forms four covalent bonds, giving chains, rings and complex structures. EN of carbon = 2.5 → covalent, not ionic. Other elements: H (almost always), O, S, N, P, halogens, metals.
  • Carbon compounds that are NOT organic: CO, CO₂, CS₂, metal carbonates and bicarbonates, HCN, metal carbides (no functional groups; different properties).
  • Distinctive features: catenation (carbon–carbon chains/rings); ~5 million organic compounds; isomerism (same molecular formula, different structures); homologous series with similar behavior; large complex molecules (starch (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ; proteins thousands to a million in mass); slow reactions (covalent bonds); mostly water-insoluble but soluble in organic solvents (ether, benzene, acetone, methanol, ethanol, CCl₄, chloroform, ethyl acetate, petroleum ether).
  • Daily life: food, metabolism, clothing, soaps, paints, fuels, medicines, DDT, hormones, steroids, paper, perfumes, explosives, refrigerants, neurotransmitters.

Key notes

  • Phenylalanine: "ph" sounds like F (don't confuse with P for proline); alanine with a phenyl (benzene) group. Tryptophan: its N–H could H-bond, yet it is still nonpolar/hydrophobic (large aromatic indole group). Proline: the only one whose side chain loops back to the backbone N (no H on its N in a peptide).
  • Cysteine: mildly polar S–H; forms disulfide bridges (covalent S–S, by oxidation) important for tertiary/quaternary structure. Tyrosine: phenylalanine with an OH at the para position (OH is small vs. the large benzyl group). Serine: alanine with OH; very hydrophilic. Threonine: serine + methyl (3 groups: CH, CH₃, OH).
  • Asparagine/Glutamine: terminal amide (NH₂ next to C=O) is neutral, not basic, but polar and H-bonding; Gln = Asn + one extra CH₂. Likewise Glu = Asp + CH₂. Acidic AAs = Asn/Gln with NH₂ swapped for OH (carboxyl).
  • Lysine: simple basic amino acid with a terminal NH₂; picks up H⁺ at physiological pH. Arginine: its guanidinium nitrogens share electrons by resonance, so only one N acts as the base. Histidine: aromatic imidazole ring; the N without a π bond (lone pair not in resonance) is the basic one.
  • Glycine: R group is just H; the only achiral amino acid. Alanine: CH₃. Valine: isopropyl. Leucine: isobutyl. Isoleucine: sec-butyl (isomer of leucine). Methionine: contains sulfur (still nonpolar).
  • Arginine is the most basic. Histidine: imidazole ring, can gain/lose H⁺ near pH 7.4, so it is common in enzyme active sites; precursor of histamine.
  • Aspartic acid/glutamic acid = protonated forms; aspartate/glutamate = deprotonated, negative forms found at body pH. Glutamate = MSG (umami).

10. Peptides & Peptide Bond

  • Peptide bond = covalent (substituted amide) bond between the carboxyl C of one amino acid and the amino N of the next. Formed by condensation (dehydration), releasing H₂O; reversed by hydrolysis. Linked amino acids are called residues.
  • Naming: start at the N-terminal, end at the C-terminal. Example: Ser-Tyr-Cys = Seryl-Tyrosyl-Cysteine (all but the last get "-yl").
  • Peptide size: dipeptide, tripeptide, tetrapeptide, oligopeptide; polypeptide/protein ≈ 50+ residues.
  • Optical activity: alpha carbon is chiral (4 different groups) in all except glycine. Proteins contain only L-amino acids. (L: NH₂ on the left when COOH is at top; D: NH₂ on the right.)
  • Amphoterism: can act as acid or base. In neutral solution they exist as a zwitterion (dipolar ion; H⁺ moves from –COOH to –NH₂).
  • Number prefixes: mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5, hexa 6, hepta 7, octa 8, ennea 9, deca 10, hendeca 11, dodeca 12, hexadeca 16, icosa 20, hecto 100, chili 1000, poly = several. Tripeptide example Val-Ala-Gly (VAG); dipeptide Gly-Gly (GG).
  • A linear peptide has a free –NH₂ at the N-terminal residue (written on the left, numbered 1) and a free –COOH at the C-terminal residue. Example: Ala-Pro-Tyr-Met-Gly-Lys-Gly. A parenthesis, e.g., Ala-Pro-(Met, Tyr)-Gly-Lys-Gly, means that part of the sequence is unknown.
  • Naming: peptides are named as acyl derivatives of the C-terminal amino acid, which keeps its full name: Ala-Tyr-Gly = alanyltyrosylglycine. The Gly-Gly image shows glycylglycine + H₂O.
  • Hormone peptides (nonapeptides): Oxytocin (Cys-Tyr-Ile-Gln-Asn-Cys-Pro-Leu-Gly-NH₂): childbirth, lactation, bonding. Vasopressin (Cys-Tyr-Phe-Gln-Asn-Cys-Pro-Arg-Gly-NH₂): water retention by kidneys, raises blood pressure. Both have a Cys–Cys disulfide bridge and an amidated C-terminus (–NH₂).

11. Protein Structure

  • Simple proteins yield only amino acids on hydrolysis; conjugated proteins yield amino acids plus other components. Protein function is determined by its unique amino acid sequence and structure. Amino acids are also used to make neurotransmitters and hormones.
  • Secondary structure: regular 3-D structure held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O oxygen and the N–H hydrogen of the backbone; examples: α-helix and β-pleated sheet.
LevelDescription
PrimaryAmino acid sequence
SecondaryLocal folding (e.g., α-helix, β-sheet) via hydrogen bonds
TertiaryOverall 3-D shape held by H-bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds (e.g., insulin)
QuaternaryTwo or more polypeptide chains together (e.g., hemoglobin, 4 chains)
TypeTraitsExamples
FibrousFiber-like, structural, water-insolubleCollagen (tendons, ligaments, bone), keratin, fibroin
GlobularSpherical, water-soluble; enzymes, binding, transportMyoglobin (O₂ in muscle), carboxypeptidase A (digestive enzyme), IgG (antibody, ~75% of serum antibodies), hemoglobin (O₂/CO₂ in RBCs)

Red liquid in cooked chicken is mostly myoglobin (or bone marrow), not blood. Insulin: pancreatic islets of Langerhans hormone regulating blood glucose; lack causes diabetes.

Denaturation

Loss of function through change in secondary, tertiary or quaternary structure (weak noncovalent bonds and S–S bonds break; peptide bonds/primary structure stay). Denatured proteins are less soluble and often precipitate. Causes: heat, detergents, mechanical action (whipping), certain acids or bases, pH change. Example: egg white.

Blood terms

  • Whole blood: RBCs, WBCs, platelets and plasma. Plasma: liquid part (~55% of volume): water, proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, wastes. Serum: plasma without clotting factors (what remains after clotting).
  • Erythrocytes (RBC): carry O₂ to tissues, CO₂ back to lungs; leukocytes (WBC): immunity; platelets: clotting; hemoglobin: red O₂-carrying protein; hemolymph: circulatory fluid of invertebrates.

Summary: proteins are polymers of amino acids; ≤~50 residues = polypeptide; 4 levels of structure; fibrous (structural, insoluble) vs. globular (transport/binding/enzymes, soluble); proteins denature by heat, pH change and mechanical action.

Kwashiorkor (protein deficiency) mnemonic K-WASH: Kids, Water retention (edema), Albumin decreased, Swollen belly, Hair/skin changes. Also fatty liver and impaired growth.
